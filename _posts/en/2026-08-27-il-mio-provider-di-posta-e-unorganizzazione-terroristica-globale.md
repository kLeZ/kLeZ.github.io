## So it isn't terrorism, then

Of course not. Because it's hosting. And hosting works in a different way, which we've all known for twenty years.

Whoever carries or hosts other people's stuff is not liable for that stuff until they have actual knowledge of it and refuse to act. In Europe, article 6 of the Digital Services Act tells you so. In the United States, section 512 of the DMCA and section 230 of the Communications Decency Act tell you so. It's the principle half the internet stands on, including all the American services that same administration uses to tweet, or truth-burp.

Sanctions law knows nothing of these mechanisms. No *notice and takedown*. No distinction between those who know and those who don't. No way to put yourself right by removing a piece of content. It has just one formula, written in 2001 with people wiring money to jihadists in mind, which catches anyone who provides *goods and services* in support of an act of terrorism.

A hosting contract, to the letter, is a provision of services.

Which means, in a nutshell, that the internet's liability regime can be dodged by switching tools. You don't go after the content, you sanction the infrastructure. No judge, no hearing, and without even having jurisdiction over the target.

The scope of that definition, if you take it seriously, is monstrous. Riseup hosts the very same user base and is based in the United States. Tor makes the anonymity tool par excellence. Proton and Tuta sell encrypted email to anyone who pays. Self-run Mastodon instances moderate sod all. The Internet Archive preserves claims of responsibility of every stripe.

And Cloudflare, whose market share is orders of magnitude larger, has been keeping up infrastructure for far more compromised groups for years.

Guess who's not on the list.

## How to strangle a server without touching it

This part should interest you even if you couldn't give a toss about A/I, because it concerns your server.

The SDN list needs no Italian cooperation whatsoever to do damage. The technical and financial layer of the internet is almost entirely American, so they already have the lever in their hands.

The domains `autistici.org`, `inventati.org` and `noblogs.org` sit under `.org`, run by Public Interest Registry, a US body headquartered in Virginia (the free, decentralised internet, yes). What happens when an OFAC-bound registry meets a listed party we saw two months ago: in July 2026 Telegram's entire `t.me` domain ended up in `serverHold` because **one** t.me address appeared as a contact in the entry of a sanctioned VPN service. The `.me` registry, American, switched the lot off.

And it couldn't do anything else, because EPP doesn't allow you to suspend a single path inside a domain. Either you block the whole name or you block nothing. *Overblocking* by design, not by malice: the protocol was born that way in 2004 and nobody ever thought about it, because nobody imagined it would become a foreign policy tool.

The TLS certificates for half the non-commercial internet are issued by Let's Encrypt, run by ISRG, American. Since June 2026 the *subscriber agreement* contains an explicit declaration: you warrant that you are not a party subject to sanctions restrictions. An entity on the SDN list can't sign that declaration. No signature, no certificate, no padlock, and your auntie's browser tells the world the site is dangerous.

Add to that the payment networks for donations, DDoS mitigation, and the European banks that cut ties with listed parties long before anyone forces them to.

Nobody issues an order. Nobody signs anything. Nobody takes responsibility. Quite simply, one piece at a time, the server stops existing. Not even a body: it just vanishes.

## No judge, no lawyer, no defence

Whoever ends up on the SDN list doesn't receive an indictment. They receive a public entry a few lines long and an administrative file that can also rest on classified material they won't let you see. Defend yourself against that, if you can.

The way out is a *delisting* petition to OFAC. In other words an appeal to the very office that stitched you up, with no binding deadlines, so they can take three years and be fine with that. A real challenge goes through the federal courts of the District of Columbia, with a standard of review so deferential to the executive that when national security is involved the judge practically apologises for the intrusion.

Mind you, though: American judges have already said the mechanism is rotten. In *KindHearts v. Geithner* (2009) a court in Ohio recognised a due process violation for a non-profit designated under none other than Executive Order 13224. In *Al Haramain v. Department of the Treasury* (2011) the Ninth Circuit ruled that an asset freeze is a seizure for Fourth Amendment purposes and that the Fifth Amendment requires them to tell you why and let you respond. The government decided not to take it to the Supreme Court, which means it knew perfectly well how it would end.

Sounds like good news, doesn't it?

It isn't. Because both cases concerned entities based in the United States. And in the same ruling the Ninth Circuit notes, almost in passing, that most designated parties **are not** US citizens or entities.

That sentence is everything. The protections that saved a foundation in Oregon don't apply to foreigners without substantial ties to the country. A volunteer association in San Giuliano Terme sits squarely in that majority without remedies.

A defence exists, by all means. It costs hundreds of thousands of dollars. To be paid by people who get by on 20-euro donations.
